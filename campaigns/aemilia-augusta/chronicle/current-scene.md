# Current scene

- S02, approximately 12 BC, more than a week after Aemilia's pregnancy loss; exact day count remains loose. Aemilia is eighteen, at the Valerian domus in Rome. It is the afternoon of the second day of Aemilia's return to household duties.
- Earlier events remain active canon: Eros's disclosure caused a severe marital argument; Lucius twice physically stopped Aemilia leaving and the second restraint broke his explicit promise not to stop her again. He later admitted **“I was wrong.”** The marital breach remains unresolved.
- Aemilia lost her approximately four-month pregnancy after hours of pain and bleeding in the shared bedchamber despite attendance by Demetrios and the trusted midwife. No medical cause was established. She then remained unconscious for about a day from severe weakness/blood loss before waking.
- Pia failed to return from her first genuine day of leave and remains absent. Her reason and whereabouts/fate remain unestablished to Aemilia and Lucius.
- After grief-driven withdrawal, Cornelia's visit and a second withdrawal after sending her mother home, Aemilia returned to household duties. She now works in a changed manner: controlled and soft-voiced but without her former warmth, dimpling smiles or instinct to cushion commands. This reserve applies broadly to everyone, including Eros.
- Aemilia remains physically weakened after major blood loss, prolonged inactivity and limited eating. She is now on her second day back at household work. No fever, renewed hemorrhage or new medical complication is established.
- Earlier at midday Terentia, Lucius's stepmother and matron of the elder Messalla household, visited. During the visit Aemilia disclosed the unresolved marital breach involving Eros and Lucius, including Lucius physically stopping her twice and breaking his promise not to stop her again. Terentia did not establish a causal link between the argument/restraint and the later miscarriage, but took the broken promise and physical restraint seriously.
- Terentia advised that Aemilia need not punish Eros or her children, but mercy does not require confidence or warmth; Lucius remains responsible for his own choices. Aemilia has already told Lucius what hurt her and need not repeatedly chase him for acknowledgment. If the marriage is to recover, Lucius must eventually choose to address the breach rather than allow ordinary married life to erase it.
- Aemilia changed the subject to Valeria Tertia. Valeria is well, remains unmarried and unaware that her parents are considering Gaius Vibius Rufus as a possible husband. Marcus is investigating Rufus as a dynastic prospect while Terentia scrutinizes his temperament and motives. Terentia is cautiously favorable. No engagement has been made.
- As Terentia prepared to leave, she invited Aemilia to visit the elder Messalla household when strong enough, saying Valeria would be glad to see her. Aemilia took Terentia's offered hand and said, **“I would like that.”** Terentia departed without reopening the marital dispute.
- After Terentia left, Aemilia instinctively reached to stroke her stomach, caught herself when she realized the old pregnancy habit, and stopped the gesture. She then turned back to her household work.
- Later in the afternoon Aemilia went to find the household steward herself rather than summoning him. She asked, **“Is there any word on Pia?”** The steward said there was still no confirmed word: Pia has neither returned nor sent a message that has reached the household. Pia remains missing; no sightings, witnesses, search result, reason or fate have been established in-world. When asked if she wanted anything further done, Aemilia said, **“No I… just hope she is safe.”**
- On trying to return from the working part of the domus, Aemilia reaches a set of steps and discovers she is too weak to climb them. She stops, leans against the wall, and tells a concerned nearby worker, **“I’m just resting.”** Although not sufficiently recovered, she pushes herself to climb anyway. After several steps her legs shake, breathing becomes hard and one knee nearly folds.
- Aemilia tells the worker, **“Get Lucius.”** While the summons is carried, she rests her head against the hand braced on the wall, closes her eyes and deliberately breathes slowly. Lucius arrives promptly, dismisses unnecessary onlookers, comes close without seizing her and asks permission to carry her.
- In answer, Aemilia reaches one hand toward Lucius but does not move her feet because she fears falling. Lucius takes the offered hand, warns her before touching further, and lifts her securely from the steps rather than making her attempt another step.
- While Lucius carries her upward, Aemilia voluntarily rests her head against his chest, says nothing and closes her eyes in the warmth of being held. This is the first voluntary physical closeness she has shown him in the immediate post-loss period after the unresolved marital breach. Lucius notices the gesture but does not treat it as resolution or press for discussion.
- Aemilia gives Lucius no instruction about where to take her. Remembering her visible distress around the bed where the pregnancy loss occurred, Lucius carries her to a nearby private sitting room rather than their shared bedchamber, has the room cleared, and lowers her carefully onto a couch.
- Aemilia asks Lucius whether he figured out Publius's debts. Lucius answers that the matter with Aufidius still has loose ends and has not been forgotten; the exact outcome remains unestablished. When he tells her she will not be getting up to inspect the papers, Aemilia replies, **“I brought the burden on you.”**
- Still holding his hand, Aemilia apologizes: **“I’m sorry. I took you from your work.”** Lucius rejects the apology without withdrawing from her, telling her that his work can wait and that answering when his wife calls for him is not a burden she owes him an apology for.
- Aemilia then asks, **“How do you do it? Carry on after you lose a child?”** Lucius answers from his own history rather than offering easy consolation: four of the seven children born to enslaved women before his adult marriage to Aemilia died in infancy or early childhood. He tells her that he did not discover a way to stop grieving; duties simply continued to arrive, and in time grief ceased occupying every moment without disappearing. He does not equate his experience with Aemilia's bodily loss and tells her she does not have to know yet how to carry it.
- Aemilia falls silent again, unable to find anything to say. Lucius does not press her for an answer, fill the silence, or withdraw. He remains beside the couch holding her hand, allowing the silence itself to stand between them without treating it as rejection or reconciliation.
- Aemilia then lowers herself fully onto the couch and retreats again into silence and stillness. Lucius recognizes the return of the withdrawn manner that marked the weeks after the loss. He does not force speech, touch beyond what she permits, or demand that the brief closeness continue; he remains nearby rather than abandoning her to it.
- Seeing Aemilia withdrawing again, Lucius decides not to continue concealing Eros's later approach. He tells Aemilia that during the weeks after the loss, Eros came to him privately and made it clear she was offering the kind of sexual access that had existed between them before Aemilia's adult marriage. Lucius states that he understood the offer and was tempted, but refused and did not have sex with Eros because his fidelity promise to Aemilia still bound him. He does not characterize Eros's motive as established, nor present her enslaved status as permitting a freely equal sexual proposition. Aemilia has now been told of the encounter for the first time.
- Aemilia sits up abruptly enough that dizziness forces her to pause, then challenges him: **“You are telling me this to absolve your conscience?”** Lucius does not deny that keeping the encounter secret has weighed on him, but rejects the idea that disclosure earns absolution. He tells her that he is speaking because concealing something involving Eros after their earlier breach would make another secret between them, and that Aemilia is entitled to judge what the disclosure means without being required to comfort or forgive him.
- Aemilia asks, **“You are still allowing her to have access to you?”** Lucius distinguishes ordinary household access from private access: Eros remains in his household and is mother to two of his surviving children, but after her approach he has not knowingly received her alone or permitted a repeat of that private intimacy. He acknowledges that he should have made that boundary explicit rather than leaving Aemilia to discover the issue only now.
- Aemilia presses, **“You permit her to come to you at all.”** Lucius accepts the correction rather than hiding behind the private/public distinction. He says that he has still allowed Eros ordinary access to him as a member of his household and mother of his children, and acknowledges that after her sexual approach he chose containment rather than removing her access altogether. He does not yet promise banishment, sale, punishment, or separation; he asks Aemilia what boundary she believes should exist, making clear that he is listening rather than treating her concern as irrational.
- Aemilia answers, **“You do not have to keep your fidelity. Be with whoever you want. You are master of your life. I cannot make you do anything.”** Lucius refuses to treat this as a release from his fidelity promise. He acknowledges the structural truth that Aemilia cannot compel him as he can exercise authority within the household, but says his promise was voluntarily given and is therefore his obligation rather than something she must enforce. He states that he does not want other women and will not use Aemilia's grief, anger, or resignation as permission to abandon the promise. He does not demand that she trust him merely because he says this.
- Aemilia raises her voice and says, **“You will still not deny yourself Eros. She may not warm your bed but she is tearing us apart. You still refuse to see it!”** Lucius does not answer by defending Eros or by reducing the issue to sexual fidelity. He accepts that he has been treating the problem as though refusing sex and controlling private access were sufficient, while Aemilia is telling him Eros's continuing place in his personal orbit has itself become part of the marital fracture. He stops short of deciding Eros's fate unilaterally in the same breath and asks Aemilia to tell him plainly whether she wants Eros removed from his personal service/access, removed from the domus, or something else, because he will not pretend any longer that the existing arrangement is neutral.
- Aemilia answers that she does not want Eros able to come to Lucius whenever she pleases, especially while he is alone. She rejects his invocation of Fortunata and Felix as a reason for Eros's privileged direct access, arguing that matters concerning the children can be carried to him by others. She accuses Lucius of giving Eros an exceptional degree of personal access that feeds Eros's sense of importance and says bitterly that he grants Eros something he grants no one else, **“Not even me.”** Lucius accepts the practical boundary: Eros will no longer have discretionary direct access to him or be received alone; necessary household or child-related matters can pass through ordinary household channels or be raised in the presence of others. More painfully, he does not dismiss Aemilia's comparison as jealousy: he acknowledges that he has required forms, restraint, and deference from his wife while allowing an enslaved former sexual partner an informal route into his presence, and that this contradiction has helped create the very significance he claimed Eros did not possess.
- Aemilia says Eros's place makes the pregnancy loss harder to bear. She tells Lucius that he repeatedly invokes his existing children in defense of Eros's access and admits she had believed that if she could give him a child, things between them would be different. She contrasts her own grief over their lost pregnancy with Eros approaching Lucius sexually during that grief and with Lucius presenting his refusal as though it should reassure her, saying she would have been furious had she known then. Lucius does not answer by comparing the lost pregnancy to his surviving children or by demanding credit for refusing Eros. He says Aemilia never owed him a child as the price of becoming secure in his household or marriage, and acknowledges that his repeated appeal to Eros's children has made the surviving children function in the argument as proof of a bond Aemilia has just lost the chance to establish with him. He also concedes that telling her merely that he turned Eros down cannot make the approach itself harmless and that her anger would have been justified.
- Aemilia clarifies, **“I don’t want a child because I’m expected to. I want a child because I want you to look at me the way you do her, to defend me the way you do her.”** Lucius does not answer by claiming he feels the same toward Aemilia and Eros. He acknowledges the more damaging point: whatever he believes privately about Aemilia's importance, his conduct has repeatedly made Eros's place something he explains and protects while Aemilia has had to argue for her own security and boundaries. He tells Aemilia that no child should have been necessary to make him defend his wife, and that if she has come to believe motherhood is what would make him choose her visibly, then his actions—not her failure to bear a surviving child—have taught her that lesson. He does not ask for immediate forgiveness or insist that words alone correct it.
- Aemilia presses the concrete failures: **“And yet you do not rebuke her for calling me weak. Or cast her out when she comes to you while I bleed the loss of our child.”** Lucius does not dispute either point. He acknowledges that when Eros described Aemilia as sheltered and likely to yield, he concentrated on explaining Eros's position rather than plainly defending his wife's standing, and that this failure helped precipitate the quarrel. He also acknowledges that after Eros later sought renewed sexual access during Aemilia's grief, he refused the sex but imposed no consequence proportionate to what the approach meant for the marriage. He tells Aemilia he will not pretend these were acts of protection simply because he did not sleep with Eros. He stops short of instantly selling or expelling Eros in anger, but makes clear that the newly established restriction on Eros's access is a consequence and that he will decide any further household disposition without treating Eros's children as an automatic veto against action.
- Aemilia tells Lucius, **“We may be married 6 years, but you have only had a wife for 5 months.”** Lucius accepts the distinction rather than invoking the legal duration of the marriage. He acknowledges that for most of the six years he was guardian-like husband to a child he deliberately refused to treat as an adult sexual wife, whereas only in the recent adult phase has he had to learn what it means to live with Aemilia as a grown wife with claims, desires, anger, judgment and expectations of him. He admits that he has sometimes continued acting as though he alone defines the terms and Aemilia must grow into them, and that the last months have exposed how inadequate that habit is for the marriage they actually chose. He does not use his inexperience as an excuse.
- Aemilia answers, **“It cost me my child.”** Lucius does not accept an unestablished medical causal claim as fact, nor does he dismiss the emotional truth behind it. He says he does not know whether the quarrel, his restraint, or anything he did caused the pregnancy loss, because Demetrios and the midwife established no cause. He refuses to lie to Aemilia by claiming certainty either way. At the same time, he acknowledges that his actions cost her safety and trust on the very night their child was lost, so the breach and the loss are now inseparable in her memory even if their medical relationship remains unknown. He does not ask her to absolve him of that.
- In speaking of the lost pregnancy, Lucius inadvertently uses the masculine pronoun. Aemilia's eyes fill with tears for the first time in weeks and she asks, **“It was a boy?”** Because the sex of the lost child was already established in canon as unknown, Lucius cannot truthfully confirm it; he immediately tells her he does not know and that his use of “him” was a slip, not hidden knowledge. He does not try to stop her tears or touch her without invitation.
- Aemilia lowers her gaze to her hands in her lap while the tears continue. Lucius remains beside her without filling the silence, demanding eye contact, or reaching for her. He lets the first visible tears of her grief in weeks come without turning them into reconciliation or asking anything of her.
- Still looking down, Aemilia says, **“I miss Pia.”** Lucius does not reduce Pia to a servant whose absence can simply be filled by another attendant. He acknowledges that Aemilia misses her and, because no confirmed word has arrived, does not offer false reassurance about Pia's safety or return. He remains close and quiet, leaving Aemilia room to speak about Pia if she chooses.
- Aemilia says, **“I wanted to free her.”** Lucius treats this as a serious statement of intended manumission rather than a sentimental figure of speech. He does not presume that Pia is dead or that the intention is now impossible; with Pia's fate unknown, he answers that if Pia returns, Aemilia may still decide what she wishes to do for her. He does not use Pia's legal status to diminish Aemilia's attachment.
- Aemilia says, **“I wasn’t ready. I still need her.”** Lucius does not shame the admission or turn Pia's prospective freedom into abandonment. He tells Aemilia that needing Pia and wanting freedom for her are not mutually exclusive: if Pia returns, freedom would not itself require Pia to vanish from Aemilia's life. He leaves Pia's own wishes unresolved rather than assuming she would remain in the household after manumission.
- Aemilia says, **“I really hope she escaped and is happy than in danger or… dead.”** Lucius hears the difficult preference beneath it: Aemilia would rather Pia have deliberately fled the household and found safety than have vanished through violence, coercion, accident, or death. He does not condemn Aemilia for preferring Pia's freedom even if it meant losing her service and companionship, nor does he pretend that escape would be safe or easy for an enslaved woman. He says that if those are the alternatives, he too would rather learn Pia chose to run and survived. Pia's actual fate remains unknown in-world.
- Aemilia admits the conflict behind her earlier refusal to order further action: **“I’m afraid of the truth if we tried to find her. If she ran then it hurts she would leave me. But if we don’t try to find her when she is hurt or worse, then I couldn’t forgive myself.”** Lucius accepts both fears without treating them as contradictory. He says they can look for Pia in order to learn whether she is in danger, not in order to punish or drag her back if she deliberately fled. He offers to have inquiries made discreetly and makes the distinction explicit: if they find evidence Pia chose escape and is safe, the search can stop there; if she is hurt or being held, they can act. No search has yet been ordered or carried out as a result of this conversation.
- Aemilia asks, **“What if the other enslaved hear about it and run too?”** Lucius does not dismiss the concern. He acknowledges that if it became known throughout the household that a fugitive could leave and the master would deliberately decline recapture, others might read that as a practical opening and household authority could be weakened. He therefore distinguishes private mercy from a public rule: any inquiry into Pia can be kept narrow and discreet, and if Pia is found to have escaped safely they need not advertise either her whereabouts or the decision not to pursue her. He does not claim secrecy eliminates all risk, nor turn the possibility into an argument that Pia must therefore be recaptured. No search has yet been ordered; Aemilia still has not accepted or rejected the proposed inquiry.
- Aemilia looks down and says, **“I thought she wanted to see my child be born.”** The remark ties Pia's disappearance directly into Aemilia's pregnancy grief: Aemilia had imagined Pia remaining beside her through the pregnancy and birth, and Pia vanished before either could happen. Lucius does not claim to know what Pia privately wanted or reinterpret her disappearance as proof she did not care. He tells Aemilia that Pia's absence cannot establish that her earlier care or apparent anticipation was false; they still do not know why she disappeared.

Aemilia promised no retaliation and said she and Lucius do not wish to separate Eros from Fortunata/Felix; no manumission promise was made.

- During the afternoon/evening, Eros privately told Lucius substantially the whole conversation. Lucius knows about Eros's inability to meaningfully refuse his former sexual access, her conditional wish for freedom, the Menophilus/Chryseros/Thais allegations, Aemilia's former jealousy, and Aemilia's statement that the only person she has to fear is her husband. Eros also conveyed her judgment that Aemilia is kind and sincere but sheltered and unlikely to prevail if Lucius says no. Lucius did not retaliate.
- Publius workshop crisis remains active; exact outcome of today's creditor deadline remains unestablished on-screen and must not be assumed.
- At bedtime Lucius confronted Aemilia over the fear statement. Aemilia feels betrayed and humiliated by Eros because she had approached her hoping to understand enslaved lives, improve what she could, and lay down jealousy toward the woman who shared Lucius's bed and bore his children before Aemilia.
- Lucius explained Eros went to him because he holds final authority and Eros does not yet trust Aemilia's goodwill or authority enough to rely on it alone. Aemilia said what wounds her is that Lucius defends Eros and tried to leave. Lucius physically stopped her by the arm, then released her when Aemilia explained she fears not bodily harm but that displeasing him will result in renewed exclusion from his company and affection.
- Lucius promised not to use withdrawal of his company/marital affection as discipline. He later explicitly told Aemilia that if she still wished to leave the room he would not stop her again.
- Aemilia argued that Eros confides in Lucius in a way Aemilia never could, that Lucius defends an enslaved woman when Aemilia was trying to improve Eros's life, and that Aemilia feels condemned as cruel merely for existing in a privileged life she did not choose. She compared her father's arrangement of her marriage and Lucius's authority to ownership. Lucius rejected literal equivalence between her constrained elite womanhood and Eros's enslavement while acknowledging the marriage was arranged and his formal authority is greater.
- Aemilia accused Lucius of letting Eros hurt his wife because Eros's life is worse, and said Eros clearly has his ear and heart. She turned for the door. Lucius, angered by the accusation that Eros has his heart, broke his explicit promise not to stop Aemilia again: he drove the door shut and forced Aemilia back against it, avoiding her pregnant abdomen but physically confining her. This is a serious marital boundary breach.
- While breathing heavily against the door, Aemilia argues with greater precision: **“I promised her nothing, she had no cause to come to you. I didn't promise her that she would stay with her children or her freedom. I did not pretend I had that authority. Instead of sending her away you listened. Instead of telling her she undermined me you brought an accusation.”**
- This exposes an overstatement in Lucius's earlier defense. Aemilia did not promise Eros freedom or guaranteed status for her children and explicitly made no manumission promise. She did promise no retaliation and said she and Lucius do not wish to separate Eros from Fortunata/Felix, but she did not pretend to possess final authority over manumission or household ownership decisions.
- Lucius concedes the distinction. He does not agree Eros had **no** cause to speak to him: Eros disclosed allegations about Menophilus and other household authority, her own wishes concerning freedom/children, and the fact of Lucius's former sexual access without meaningful refusal, all matters directly involving Lucius's authority/responsibility. But he acknowledges Eros also repeated Aemilia's personal jealousy and fear statement, which were not necessary to report the Menophilus allegations or request future consideration, and that this did undermine Aemilia's attempt to establish an independent relationship of confidence with Eros.
- Lucius also recognizes that he came into the bedchamber leading with **“You are afraid of me?”** rather than first telling Aemilia that Eros had breached her confidence. In doing so he treated Eros's report as evidence to interrogate Aemilia before acknowledging the injury done to his wife. He does not characterize Eros's entire disclosure as malicious, but can no longer honestly dismiss Aemilia's complaint as mere jealousy.
- CURRENT MOMENT: Aemilia remains at the closed bedchamber door after Lucius physically prevented her leaving. Her argument has forced a substantive concession: Eros had legitimate reasons to bring some matters to Lucius, but not every private confidence was necessary, and Lucius mishandled the disclosure by confronting Aemilia first instead of recognizing that Eros had undermined her. The separate, serious issue of Lucius having broken his promise and physically confined Aemilia remains unresolved.

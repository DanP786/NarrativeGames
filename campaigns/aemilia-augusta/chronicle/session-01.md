# Session 01

## Opening and six-year development
18 BC, Rome. Twelve-year-old Aemilia marries twenty-four-year-old Lucius Valerius Messalla and enters the Valerian household with a substantial dowry and attendants from her natal home. On their wedding night Lucius tells her he will not consummate the marriage while she is a child. He remains the formal household authority but begins an unusual program of education, giving Aemilia access to Latin and Greek letters, accounts, practical law, patronage, politics and increasingly serious household business.

Across roughly six years Aemilia grows from a docile elite girl into an educated and observant young mistress of the domus. She becomes deeply attached to Lucius and the Valerian household, while the structural inequality of the marriage remains real. She develops particular affection for Lucius's surviving children by enslaved women: Hilarus, Fortunata and Felix. Lucius acknowledges them and shows them paternal affection. His former sexual access to their mothers and other enslaved women is understood within the coercive structure of slavery rather than as freely consensual relationships.

Aemilia brings the Greek physician Demetrios through the household and follows his practical recommendations on water, food storage, waste, washing, linens, illness and childbirth cleanliness. She turns these recommendations into durable household routines and observes fewer illnesses afterward, strengthening her practical authority as domina.

## Adulthood, marriage and pregnancy
Near Aemilia's eighteenth birthday Lucius tells her that he loves her. Aemilia asks for fidelity once their marriage becomes fully adult, and Lucius promises that from her eighteenth birthday onward he will seek no sexual access to Eros or any other woman. On her eighteenth birthday the marriage enters its adult phase by mutual choice; the night itself remains undescribed. Their subsequent private married life becomes frequent and affectionate.

Several months later Aemilia misses her expected cycle. Demetrios and a trusted experienced midwife examine the evidence and judge pregnancy strongly established by the standards available to them. The pregnancy progresses without an established complication. At approximately three months Aemilia is only slightly showing; her body and clothing have begun to change, fatigue is established, and she prefers softer low-tension hairstyles. Pia remains her trusted personal attendant and the first servant formally told.

At a Valerian family birthday gathering the pregnancy becomes openly known among close relatives. Marcus Valerius Messalla learns that Aemilia is approximately three months pregnant and concedes to Lucius, 'I was wrong,' about the necessity of haste in producing an heir. Terentia warmly receives the news. Aemilia publicly answers an uncomfortable remark about Lucius's enslaved children by resting a hand on her slight belly and saying, 'His children are dear to me. All of them.'

Later Aemilia learns more fully that, during the six years he maintained the childhood boundary with her, Lucius used his ownership power to summon six adult enslaved women in total, including Eros and Daphne. Lucius does not describe them as lovers and states plainly, 'They were women of my household. I had the power to summon them, and I used it.' Aemilia distinguishes those years from their adult marriage and ultimately tells him, 'Ok, I will forgive what came before.' She asks whether his fidelity promise will hold through pregnancy, childbirth, illness or periods when intimacy is medically unwise; Lucius answers unequivocally that it will.

## Valeria Tertia and Gaius Vibius Rufus
Aemilia privately proposes Gaius Vibius Rufus, a wealthy approximately twenty-two-year-old equestrian contact of Lucius, as a possible husband for Lucius's thirteen-year-old half-sister Valeria Tertia. Aemilia judges Rufus kind, clever, perceptive, commercially capable and more ambitious for information than office. Lucius recognizes the strategic value of Rufus's money, credit, contracts and information network but insists that Valeria is his sister rather than a contract: if Valeria dislikes Rufus, Terentia finds him unsuitable, or Rufus treats her merely as a route into the Valerii, the matter ends.

Aemilia and Lucius arrange a deliberately deniable encounter at their domus: Terentia and Valeria visit as family while Rufus independently comes on legitimate business. Marcus Valerius Messalla is not told. Terentia quickly understands Aemilia's purpose and becomes cautiously favorable, though Rufus's equestrian rather than senatorial status is a serious dynastic obstacle. Valeria and Rufus are not told they are being observed as a possible match.

Valeria tests Rufus by asking him to locate an unidentified Greek poem about Atalanta remembered from a tutor. Rufus does not bluff knowledge or patronize her; he questions her seriously and says that if he finds a purchasable copy he will tell her the price before buying it because its worth should be her choice. The poem remains unresolved and provides a natural reason for future contact. Lucius also draws Rufus into business discussion, allowing Terentia and Valeria to see how he reasons about information, independent reports, credit, contracts, reputation and motives.

In private Aemilia teaches Valeria to notice what people gain from interactions and to notice power even where formal authority is unequal. She tells Valeria that Lucius has the final say in their household, but that this does not mean Aemilia possesses no power of her own. She encourages Valeria to judge a prospective husband by how he reacts when his authority is challenged. Valeria distills the lesson as learning when and how to disagree and how to win an argument without making a husband feel defeated. Aemilia tells her, 'Now you're getting it. Lucius would be proud.' When Valeria asks whether Lucius would teach her directly, Aemilia promises to ask him.

## Closing beat
After supper the guests leave and Aemilia and Lucius retire. Aemilia calls it 'one of my less subtle days' and honestly recounts what she taught Valeria. Lucius is proud of Valeria's conclusion and agrees in principle to teach his sister practical social judgment discreetly, recognizing that his father would be furious at framing such lessons around a wife's ability to challenge or influence her husband.

Preparing for bed, Aemilia asks Lucius, 'Am I a good wife?' Lucius answers yes. He tells her that her value as his wife does not lie in thoughtless obedience: she is loyal to their household, thinks seriously, tells him when she believes he is wrong, recognizes where final authority lies, and has become someone whose judgment he wants before important decisions. The session ends with the couple alone in their private room, Aemilia approximately three months pregnant, the Rufus-Valeria possibility still exploratory and undisclosed, and Lucius willing to teach Valeria some of what he taught Aemilia.
